Security researcher Xusheng Li found that Lenovo's ThinkPlus FU100 fingerprint USB stick, marketed as an AES-256-encrypted device, contains only a standard unencrypted SD card inside its case. The fingerprint reader merely acts as a physical gatekeeper that toggles the SD card's connection, providing no cryptographic protection of the data. Follow-up analysis by the same researcher also found hardcoded keys, exposed passwords, and hidden accounts in other 'secure' USB products.

**Background**: Hardware-encrypted USB drives are supposed to encrypt data on the device itself, so that even if the flash memory is removed, the data remains unreadable without the correct credentials. In contrast, software encryption relies on the host computer to encrypt files, and a device that merely gates access to unencrypted storage offers no protection against physical extraction. Fingerprint authentication on a USB drive is often assumed to imply encryption, but as this case shows, it may only control access at the connection level.

In his Jepsen analysis of ParticleDB 1.1.4, Kyle Kingsbury argues that LLM-generated code and documentation erode trust in software and the organizations that produce it, because the signals engineers once used to judge a mature system are now easier to counterfeit. He asks what maintainers can do to ensure correct software and accurate documentation, and how they can then demonstrate that quality to users. This matters because the software industry has long relied on informal trust signals—polished docs, clean APIs, active maintainers—to decide what to adopt, and if LLMs can cheaply fake those signals, engineers may start treating every new project with suspicion. That shift could disadvantage honest new projects and raise the cost of evaluating dependencies across the whole ecosystem. The quote comes from the 'LLM tradeoffs' section of Jepsen's ParticleDB 1.1.4 report, which tested versions 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.11.rc-22 on Debian 13 nodes in containers and EC2 VMs. Kingsbury frames the problem as two-sided: maintainers must both do the work of ensuring correctness and find credible ways to prove it.

🔗 [Source](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/1/kyle-kingsbury/)

rss · Simon Willison · Nov 1, 16:49

**Background**: Kyle Kingsbury (also known as Aphyr) runs Jepsen, a consultancy that stress-tests distributed databases and queues by injecting network partitions, crashes, and clock skew to uncover data loss and isolation violations. His reports are widely read as a gold standard for independent verification of database claims. Large language models can now generate plausible-looking code and documentation at near-zero cost, which makes it harder to tell genuine engineering maturity from a convincing imitation.
