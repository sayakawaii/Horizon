New research indicates that deep-sea ecosystems in the northern Gulf of Mexico show minimal recovery sixteen years after the 2010 Deepwater Horizon oil spill, with deep-water species still struggling across hundreds of miles. The findings highlight the long-lasting damage of deep-sea oil spills and raise questions about the effectiveness of dispersants and the accountability of companies like BP, potentially influencing future offshore drilling regulations and restoration efforts. Researchers stopped short of directly concluding that the oil spill caused the declines, and the use of dispersants such as Corexit—which was injected at depth to break up the oil—remains controversial because it may have sunk oil to the seafloor rather than eliminating it.

🔗 [Source](https://e360.yale.edu/digest/deepwater-horizon-deep-sea-recovery)

hackernews · speckx · Oct 20, 15:59 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50168357)

**Background**: The Deepwater Horizon spill began on April 20, 2010, when the BP-operated Macondo well blew out, releasing an estimated 4.9 million barrels of oil into the Gulf of Mexico over 87 days. In an unprecedented response, BP injected massive amounts of the chemical dispersant Corexit at the wellhead, a mile below the surface, to break up the oil. Dispersants do not remove oil; they break it into smaller droplets that can sink and mix into the water column, potentially affecting deep-sea ecosystems. Previous spills like Ixtoc I in 1979 had already shown that exposed, high-energy coastal habitats recover faster than sheltered deep-sea ones.

eMudhra's intermediate certificate authority private keys were discovered to be trivially weak, with each key being a single byte (one is literally '4'), exposing a significant CA compromise. The flaw was traced to a KGC script used on a specific day to create ECC issuers, according to eMudhra's explanation in a Mozilla bug report. This incident highlights uneven revocation protections across browsers: Mozilla mitigated it within hours for Firefox users via OneCRL, while other parts of the ecosystem lack similar infrastructure, leaving many users exposed. It underscores the risks of weak key generation in the certificate authority ecosystem and the importance of robust revocation mechanisms. The private keys for all eight compromised CAs are single-byte values, making them trivially guessable; eMudhra's preliminary assessment points to a flaw in the KGC script used that day, with original ECC keys created onboard as expected but an approved deviation to the usual KGC process. The incident also raises concerns about potentially hackable IoT devices if the weak keys were used to sign leaf certificates.

🔗 [Source](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/)

hackernews · galadran · Oct 20, 13:58 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50166579)

**Background**: A certificate authority (CA) is a trusted third party that issues digital certificates, which are used to establish secure connections (e.g., HTTPS) by binding public keys to identities. Intermediate CAs are signed by a root CA and can issue further certificates, forming a chain of trust. Private keys must be kept secret and be computationally infeasible to guess; a single-byte key offers only 256 possibilities, making it trivial to brute-force. Certificate revocation is the process of invalidating compromised certificates, and browsers implement various mechanisms like CRLs, OCSP, and proprietary systems (e.g., OneCRL, CRLite) to protect users.
