<summary><span class="hz-item-title">San Francisco Startup Sells Private Onion Futures, Testing 1958 Ban</span> <span class="hz-item-score">⭐️ 7.0/10</span></summary>

A San Francisco-based company called the San Francisco Onion Futures Company is offering private onion futures contracts directly to individual buyers, arguing it is not a 'board of trade' and therefore not covered by the 1958 Onion Futures Act. The project has drawn heavy attention on Hacker News, with 352 points and 149 comments debating its legality and historical context. The venture tests a nearly 70-year-old U.S. law that bans onion futures trading, potentially reopening a debate about whether such prohibitions still make sense in modern derivatives markets. If the loophole holds, it could encourage similar private-contract workarounds for other banned or restricted instruments, such as box office receipt futures. The company argues that 7 U.S. Code § 13-1 only prohibits onion futures contracts executed 'on or subject to the rules of any board of trade in the United States,' and that it is not an organized exchange or trading facility. It sells contracts privately to individual buyers and does not operate any exchange or secondary market, which is the core of its legal defense.

🔗 [Source](https://onionfutures.com/)

hackernews · z-mach9 · Sep 19, 04:23 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49763296)

**Background**: The Onion Futures Act was passed on August 28, 1958, after two onion traders, Sam Siegel and Vincent Kosuga, cornered the onion futures market on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in 1955. The law banned futures trading on onions and was later amended in 2010 to also ban motion picture box office receipt futures, following lobbying by the Motion Picture Association of America. Futures contracts are standardized agreements to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date, typically traded on regulated exchanges; private, non-exchange agreements are more akin to forward contracts.

<summary><span class="hz-item-title">Stanford finds two parallel neural ectoderm progenitors build the brain</span> <span class="hz-item-score">⭐️ 8.0/10</span></summary>

A Stanford study published in Nature Neuroscience used lineage tracing in mouse embryos to show that the developing brain is not built from a single common neural ectoderm progenitor, but from at least two lineage-committed progenitor populations, each restricted to forming specific brain regions. The work also led the team to develop a new in vitro technique for deriving and growing brain stem cells from human pluripotent stem cells, which had previously been very difficult. If the new in vitro brain stem cell culture method holds up, it could substantially ease future research into neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS by giving scientists a more reliable supply of relevant human neural cells. The finding also challenges the long-standing textbook model that one common neural ectoderm progenitor generates the entire brain, which could reshape how developmental neuroscientists think about regional specification. The lineage tracing was performed in mouse embryos, and the abstract frames the core question as whether a single common neural ectoderm progenitor generates the whole brain or whether multiple region-restricted progenitors exist. The in vitro protocol differentiates H1 human pluripotent stem cells into definitive ectoderm within 24 hours, then anterior neural ectoderm within another 24 hours, followed by forebrain progenitors within 48 hours; a free bioRxiv preprint from July 2025 covers related work.

🔗 [Source](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2026/09/two-separate-brains.html)

**Background**: During embryonic development, the neural ectoderm is the early tissue layer that gives rise to the entire nervous system, and its cells are called neural progenitors or neural stem cells. The classical view held that a single pool of these progenitors produces all brain regions, but fate-mapping studies have hinted that distinct progenitors may already be committed to specific regions as early as gastrulation. Understanding this process matters because neural stem cells are also the starting point for in vitro models of the brain and for studying diseases in which specific neuron types degenerate, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a disorder that kills motor neurons.

<summary><span class="hz-item-title">Rust security team warns of targeted social-engineering attacks on maintainers</span> <span class="hz-item-score">⭐️ 8.0/10</span></summary>

Adam Harvey and the crates security team published a warning on September 17, 2026 that an ongoing campaign is targeting rust-lang members and owners of popular crates, using fake video-call job or contract opportunities to trick victims into installing malware or executing attacker-supplied commands. The same technique was used in the successful supply chain attack on the arrayref crate in August 2026. Because almost all modern software depends on open source, every person with publishing rights in a dependency network is a potential entry point, so a single compromised maintainer can push malicious code to millions of downstream users. This warning turns a theoretical risk into an active, confirmed threat for the Rust ecosystem and highlights how human trust, not just code, is now the weakest link in the supply chain. The attack typically begins with an invitation to a video call framed as a job, project, or contract opportunity, after which the target is asked to install something (such as a purportedly missing audio codec) or to run a command, sometimes delivered by placing it on the clipboard. The August arrayref incident involved malicious releases such as append-only-vec@0.1.9, which was published at 2026-08-20T07:37:49Z and deleted from crates.io at 2026-08-20T09:25:24Z.

🔗 [Source](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/17/targeted-attacks-on-rustaceans/)

Rust packages, known as crates, are distributed through crates.io, the official package registry, and popular crates are maintained by a small number of people who hold publishing rights. A supply chain attack occurs when an attacker compromises one of those packages so that malicious code flows automatically into every project that depends on it, which is why the security team is warning maintainers directly.
