<summary><span class="hz-item-title">Motel-room microscope work reveals clues to the origin of plants</span> <span class="hz-item-score">⭐️ 7.0/10</span></summary>

A New York Times article describes how a researcher, working with samples scooped from a dock beside a highway and examined in an $80 motel room, identified a curious trait in the organism Paulinella — siliceous scales overlapping in opposite clockwise and counter-clockwise directions — suggesting two distinct species may have been mistaken for one. The finding offers new insight into the origin of plants rather than the origin of life, as the headline implies. Paulinella is one of the only known organisms to have independently undergone primary endosymbiosis — the process by which a free-living bacterium was captured and became a permanent organelle — which is exactly how plants acquired their chloroplasts. Understanding this rare, ongoing event gives biologists a live window into an evolutionary transition that otherwise happened billions of years ago and is normally only studied through fossils and genomes. The distinguishing trait was spotted by fresh eyes: the siliceous scales covering the organism overlapped in opposite directions in the two samples, raising the question of whether they represent two different species. The work also highlights that hand-sketching what one sees under the microscope remains a live part of scientific practice, and that the Van Etten Lab runs a Paulinella consortium inviting citizen scientists with decent microscopes to contribute samples.

🔗 [Source](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/26/science/motel-science-discovery.html)

hackernews · danso · Sep 27, 14:30 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49866951)

**Background**: Paulinella is a genus of single-celled amoeboid protists that build shells out of rows of siliceous scales and crawl using fine, thread-like pseudopods. Most species are heterotrophic, but some have acquired a photosynthetic organelle called a chromatophore through primary endosymbiosis — the same kind of event that gave rise to chloroplasts in plants and algae. Primary endosymbiosis is extremely rare: it happened once for mitochondria and once for the chloroplasts of the plant lineage, making Paulinella a uniquely valuable natural experiment.

**Background**: Flip-dot displays are electromechanical dot matrix displays used in outdoor signs, buses, and trains, where each dot is a small disc with a magnetic material that flips between black and a bright color when current is applied to a coil. They are known for their low power consumption, high visibility in sunlight, and the distinctive clicking sound they make when updating. Driving them typically involves multiplexing rows and columns with H-bridges and diodes to isolate non-driven solenoids.

<summary><span class="hz-item-title">Neovim's undo file handling deletes Vim undo history, sparking data care debate</span> <span class="hz-item-score">⭐️ 7.0/10</span></summary>

A detailed incident report describes how Neovim, when encountering an undo file it does not recognize (such as one created by Vim), deletes that file instead of preserving or warning about it. This behavior caused users to lose persistent undo history, and the issue was reportedly known before the feature was released. This incident raises serious concerns about data stewardship and backward compatibility in widely-used open-source tools, as Neovim silently deletes data created by another program on a user's machine. It affects the large community of developers who rely on persistent undo and may erode trust in Neovim's handling of user data. The deletion occurs when Neovim encounters an undo file format it does not recognize, and the change was reportedly known to break undo history for both Neovim and Vim before release. Neovim's undo files are stored separately for each edited file, mapping filesystem paths directly to undo files, so deleting them removes the entire undo tree for that file.

🔗 [Source](https://unsung.aresluna.org/they-had-no-concept-of-a-duty-of-care-to-their-users/)

hackernews · jandeboevrie · Sep 27, 14:45 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49867067)

**Background**: Vim and Neovim are popular terminal-based text editors; persistent undo allows users to save and restore undo history across editing sessions via the 'undofile' option. Vim saves undo trees in a separate undo file for each edited file, using a simple scheme that maps filesystem paths directly to undo files. Neovim is a fork of Vim that aims to be more modern and extensible, but it must maintain compatibility with Vim's file formats to avoid data loss.
